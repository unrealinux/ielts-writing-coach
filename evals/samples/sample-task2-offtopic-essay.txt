Few questions in higher education attract as much debate as the relative value of the sciences and the humanities. Some maintain that scientific research drives economic growth, while others insist that the arts are essential to a civilised society. This essay argues that both are necessary, but that science and technology deserve a larger share of public funding because their benefits are more widely shared.

The case for prioritising scientific subjects rests on the way their benefits spread. A single advance in vaccine technology or agricultural science can raise living standards for millions of people who never studied the subject themselves, whereas the benefits of a degree in philosophy are largely captured by the graduate who holds it. South Korea's decision to direct a substantial share of its education budget towards engineering in the 1960s and 1970s is often cited as one foundation of its later industrial growth, and comparable patterns appear in other economies that made similar choices. On this view, public money should follow public benefit.

The counter-argument is that a society of engineers without historians, critics or linguists would be unable to interpret its own past or hold its institutions to account. The humanities cultivate precisely the skills that democratic life depends on: the ability to read a document closely, to recognise a bad argument, to imagine how a decision will be experienced by people unlike oneself. These capacities are not luxuries; they are the infrastructure of public reasoning. It does not follow, however, that they require equal funding, since their benefits, though real, are harder to measure and slower to appear.

On balance, the argument for prioritising science is stronger, but the margin is narrower than it first appears. Scientific research produces measurable returns within a single generation, which makes it an easy case for a finance ministry to fund; the humanities produce returns that are diffuse and long-term, which makes them an easy case to cut. The danger is not that one is worthless but that funding decisions taken on the basis of measurability will systematically underfund what cannot be measured. A sensible policy would therefore protect a baseline of humanities provision while directing additional investment towards scientific and technical fields.

In conclusion, the sciences and the humanities serve different purposes and cannot be ranked on a single scale, but the wider reach of scientific benefits justifies giving them a larger share of public funding. The aim should be a balanced system in which measurable returns are pursued without abandoning the slower work of interpretation on which public reasoning depends.
